5 Early Warning Signs of a Diabetic Foot Ulcer

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Early warning signs of foot problems include unusual redness or warmth, new swelling or edema, changes in skin texture or color, persistent pain, tingling or burning sensations, and sores or wounds that fail to heal within two weeks.

Explanation

Diabetic foot ulcers are open wounds or sores that commonly develop on the bottom of the foot in people with diabetes. Left unchecked, they can lead to serious infections or even amputation. Recognizing the foot ulcers warning signs early gives you the best chance to prevent complications and protect your mobility.

Why Early Detection of Foot Ulcers Matters

According to the American Diabetes Association, nearly 1 in 4 people with diabetes will develop a foot ulcer in their lifetime. High blood sugar levels and nerve damage (neuropathy) reduce your ability to feel pain, so you may not notice small injuries. Poor circulation (peripheral arterial disease) slows healing. By spotting warning signs before a sore breaks open, you can:

Treat minor issues before they worsen

Reduce the risk of infection

Avoid hospital stays and surgery

Below are five of the most common foot ulcers warning signs to watch for if you have diabetes.

1. Unusual Redness or Warmth

Even small patches of redness or warmth can signal trouble beneath the skin's surface.

Persistent redness that doesn't fade after resting your foot

A warm "hot spot" compared to the surrounding skin

Red streaks extending from a single area

What it means• These changes often point to inflammation or early infection around a developing ulcer.• With reduced sensation from neuropathy, you might not feel pain right away.

When you see redness or warmth, gently clean the area with mild soap and water. Then pat it dry and protect it with a sterile dressing. Check again later in the day to see if the redness has spread or intensified.

2. Swelling or Edema

Swelling—especially if it's new or focused around your foot or ankle—can be a clue that fluid is accumulating around damaged tissue.

Key signs to notice:

Puffiness in the foot or lower leg that wasn't there before

A "pinch test" indent that stays visible when you press your skin

Socks or shoes feeling tighter than usual

What it means• Swelling can slow blood flow and delay healing, setting the stage for an ulcer.• In advanced cases, fluid buildup may signal an underlying infection or poor circulation.

If your foot is swollen, elevate it above heart level for 15–20 minutes, two to three times daily. If swelling persists or you notice redness, fever, or increasing pain, reach out to your healthcare provider.

3. Changes in Skin Texture or Color

Diabetes can cause your skin to become thin, dry, cracked or develop thick calluses—all of which raise ulcer risk. Watch for:

Calluses or hard, thickened patches that feel spongy underneath

Blisters, cuts or abrasions that appear without obvious injury

Skin discoloration—gray, blue or dark spots under a callus

What it means• Calluses can hide deeper tissue damage; a dark spot under a callus often suggests bleeding.• Dry, cracked skin allows bacteria to enter, leading to infection.

Regularly moisturize (avoiding areas between toes) and gently file down thickened skin with a pumice stone. Never cut or shave calluses yourself. If you spot any dark spots under a callus, treat it as an urgent warning sign.

4. Persistent Pain, Tingling or Burning

Loss of sensation in your feet (neuropathy) is common in diabetes, but you may still experience odd sensations in early nerve damage. Look out for:

New or worsening tingling ("pins and needles")

Burning pain—especially at night or after resting

Sharp or shooting pains in your toes, soles or heels

What it means• These sensations often precede ulcers by weeks or months.• Pain may alert you to tissue damage you can't yet see.

5. Sores or Wounds That Don't Heal

Perhaps the clearest foot ulcer warning sign is a sore or wound that remains open or grows larger despite basic care.

Warning features include:

A cut, blister or red spot persisting beyond two weeks

Drainage that's yellow, green or foul-smelling

Blackened (necrotic) tissue around the edges of a wound

What it means• Non-healing wounds can rapidly become infected, spreading to deeper tissues or bone.• You may need prescription antibiotics, specialized dressings or even surgical debridement.

If a wound isn't improving after a few days of clean dressings, seek medical attention right away.

Who's at Higher Risk?

While anyone with diabetes can develop a foot ulcer, some factors further increase your chances:

Poorly controlled blood sugar (high A1C)

Longstanding diabetes (more than 10 years)

Peripheral neuropathy or circulation problems

Smoking or vascular disease

How to Protect Your Feet Every Day

Preventing foot ulcers starts with daily care. Here's a simple routine:

Inspect your feet once a day for redness, swelling, cuts or blisters

Wash feet gently with lukewarm water and mild soap; pat dry

Apply a light moisturizer (avoid between toes)

Wear well-fitting shoes and clean, dry socks

Change socks daily; choose moisture-wicking materials

Never walk barefoot, even at home

Trim toenails straight across and file edges smoothly

Schedule regular check-ups with a podiatrist or diabetes specialist

By combining good foot hygiene with careful monitoring, you'll catch foot ulcers warning signs early—before small issues become serious.

When to See a Doctor

Always treat any sign of infection or non-healing wound as potentially serious. Call your healthcare provider immediately if you experience:

Red streaks or spreading redness

Fever, chills or flu-like symptoms

Increasing pain unrelieved by over-the-counter pain relievers

Numbness or quick loss of feeling in your foot

Worsening swelling or drainage from a wound

If you notice any warning sign that seems life-threatening or rapidly progressing, do not delay: seek emergency care or call your local emergency number.

Early awareness of foot ulcers warning signs can save your foot—and your health. By inspecting your feet daily, managing blood sugar, and acting on even minor changes, you'll dramatically reduce the risk of serious complications. Remember, prompt attention is your best defense. Speak to a doctor about any concerning signs or symptoms to ensure you get the right treatment quickly.
