Premature Ovarian Insufficiency: What It Means When Your Ovaries Stop Working Before 40

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) occurs when the ovaries stop functioning normally before age 40. Common symptoms include irregular or missed periods, hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes, vaginal dryness, low libido, and fertility challenges caused by declining estrogen levels.

Causes of POI include genetic conditions (such as Turner syndrome or Fragile X), autoimmune disorders, chemotherapy or radiation, ovarian surgery, and idiopathic (unknown) factors. Diagnosis typically involves blood tests measuring FSH, estradiol, and AMH levels, along with pelvic ultrasound imaging.

Treatment focuses on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) to relieve symptoms and protect long-term health, fertility planning options, bone density and cardiovascular protection, and emotional support for the psychological impact of an early diagnosis.

If you're under 40 and experiencing irregular periods, hot flashes, or other concerning symptoms, early answers matter. POI can quietly affect bone, heart, and reproductive health for years if left unaddressed, so identifying the cause now helps you protect your future and access timely treatment. Explanation

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), sometimes called premature menopause, occurs when the ovaries fail to function normally before age 40. Instead of releasing healthy eggs each month and producing typical levels of estrogen, the ovaries become less active or stop working altogether. Though it affects about 1% of women under 40, it can come as a shock—impacting fertility, hormone balance and overall health. This guide will help you understand what to expect, how it's diagnosed, and what steps you can take.

Prevalence: Affects roughly 1 in 100 women under 40.

Difference from natural menopause: In natural menopause, ovarian function ceases around age 50–52. With POI, the process happens much earlier, though ovarian activity can sometimes partially return.

Diagnosing Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

If you're younger than 40 and your periods have become irregular or stopped, your doctor will likely recommend:

Blood tests

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH): consistently high levels suggest POI

Estradiol (a form of estrogen): low levels confirm reduced ovarian activity

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH): indicates the quantity of remaining eggs

Thyroid function, prolactin and autoimmune markers

Pelvic ultrasound

Assesses ovarian size and antral follicle count

Genetic testing (in select cases)

Chromosomal analysis or Fragile X premutation testing

Treatment and Management

While there's no cure to restore ovarian function permanently, management focuses on hormone replacement, fertility planning and lifestyle measures:

1. Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)

Purpose: Replaces estrogen (and sometimes progesterone) to:

Alleviate hot flashes, vaginal dryness and mood symptoms

Protect bone and cardiovascular health

Options:

Transdermal patches, gels or oral pills

Progesterone addition if you have a uterus (to protect the uterine lining)

Duration: Often recommended until average menopause age (around 50) unless contraindicated.

2. Fertility Options

Egg donation with IVF: Most common and successful path to pregnancy

Ovarian tissue or egg freezing: For those diagnosed early and interested in future parenthood

Adoption or fostering: Alternative routes to growing your family

Consult a fertility specialist soon after diagnosis to understand timelines and success rates.

3. Bone and Heart Health

Calcium (1,000–1,200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–1,000 IU/day) supplements

Weight-bearing exercises (walking, strength training)

Avoid smoking and manage blood pressure, cholesterol

4. Emotional Support

Counseling or support groups for women facing POI

Mind-body approaches: meditation, yoga or tai chi

Open communication with partners, family and friends

Living Well with Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

Track symptoms and menstrual changes in a journal or app

Stay up to date on bone density scans (DEXA)

Maintain regular check-ups with your gynecologist or primary care doctor

Build a support network: friends, family and online communities

Educate yourself—knowledge empowers better decisions

When to Speak to a Doctor

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience:

Severe pelvic or abdominal pain

Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain or leg swelling

Signs of severe depression or thoughts of self-harm

For all other concerns—new symptoms, fertility planning or hormone adjustment—schedule an appointment with your healthcare provider. Your doctor can guide you through testing, treatment and referrals to specialists.
