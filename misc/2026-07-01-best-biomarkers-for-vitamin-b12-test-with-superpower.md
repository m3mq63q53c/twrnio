What is Vitamin B12?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a water-soluble vitamin that your body cannot make on its own. You obtain it exclusively from animal-based foods like meat, fish, eggs, and dairy, or from fortified products and supplements. Once absorbed in the small intestine with the help of a protein called intrinsic factor, B12 travels through the bloodstream to cells throughout your body.

Your body's master builder for DNA and nerves

B12 plays two essential roles. First, it helps build and repair DNA, the genetic blueprint in every cell. Second, it maintains the protective coating around nerves (myelin sheath), ensuring smooth communication between your brain and the rest of your body.

Why is Vitamin B12 important?

Vitamin B12 is essential for building red blood cells, maintaining the insulation around nerves, and synthesizing DNA in every dividing cell. Without adequate B12, your bone marrow can't produce healthy blood cells, your nervous system begins to fray, and energy metabolism stalls at the cellular level.

Your nerves and blood depend on it

When B12 levels drop below the normal range, the body first depletes its liver stores, then red blood cells grow large and fragile, causing macrocytic anemia. Fatigue, pale skin, and shortness of breath emerge as oxygen delivery falters. Nerve damage follows, producing tingling in the hands and feet, balance problems, and cognitive changes including memory loss and mood disturbance.

Older adults, strict vegetarians, and people with digestive conditions absorb B12 poorly and face higher risk. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more to support fetal brain development.

High levels are rarely harmful

Elevated B12 is uncommon and usually reflects supplementation or, occasionally, liver disease or certain blood cancers that release stored B12. The body excretes excess efficiently, so toxicity is exceptionally rare.

The long view

B12 sits at the crossroads of hematology, neurology, and metabolism. Chronic deficiency can cause irreversible nerve damage and increase homocysteine, a marker linked to cardiovascular risk. Maintaining adequate B12 across the lifespan protects brain function, energy production, and the integrity of your blood and nervous systems.

What do my Vitamin B12 results mean?

Low vitamin B12 usually reflects inadequate intake, poor absorption, or increased demand

Low values usually reflect insufficient dietary intake, malabsorption in the stomach or small intestine, or autoimmune damage to cells that produce intrinsic factor, a protein needed for B12 uptake. Strict vegetarians and vegans are at higher risk because B12 is found almost exclusively in animal foods. Older adults often absorb B12 poorly due to reduced stomach acid or atrophic gastritis. Low B12 impairs red blood cell production, leading to macrocytic anemia with large, immature cells. It also disrupts myelin formation in nerves, causing numbness, tingling, balance problems, and cognitive changes. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more B12 to support fetal brain development.

Optimal vitamin B12 supports healthy blood and nerve function

Being in range suggests adequate stores to support DNA synthesis, red blood cell maturation, and nervous system integrity. Optimal values tend to sit in the mid to upper portion of the reference range, reflecting robust reserves in the liver and other tissues.

High vitamin B12 may reflect supplementation or underlying metabolic conditions

High values usually reflect recent supplementation or, less commonly, liver disease, kidney dysfunction, or certain blood cancers that release stored B12 into circulation. Elevated B12 is rarely harmful on its own but warrants investigation of the underlying cause.
