# He Drilled into His Own Skull to Implant a Chip in His Brain. What Could Go Wrong?

Almaty, Kazakhstan, 2023

THE HARDWARE STORE drill vibrated in Michael Raduga’s right hand as he held it to the side of his bloodied head. To make sure he was drilling into the correct part of his brain, he had rigged up a system using an endoscope camera connected to a monitor, with a ring light illuminating the site on his shaved head that he had marked using a permanent marker. As millions of people learned during the Covid pandemic, just cutting one’s own hair in the mirror is tricky, especially in the back, because your brain sees your hand holding the scissors in one direction but your hand doesn’t immediately cooperate. Raduga was facing the same challenge, only instead of clipping his hair with scissors, he was drilling into his skull, and not just into his skull but through it, so that he could insert a homemade implant in his brain.

He had applied local anesthesia to the site on his head before cutting a dime-size hole using a scalpel. But the drilling—you know at the dentist, when your jaw feels weird during the drilling? He felt that through his entire body. He felt as if his skull was rattling, and there was blood all over the floor of the rented apartment, all over the old wood vanity that was his operating table.

Scattered around him on every flat surface: enamelware stock pots filled with Evian water and blood, tool-drawer pliers, cooking knives, kitchen plates with yellow flowers on them. Everything spattered with Raduga’s blood.

He had practiced on sheep heads purchased from a butcher shop. He knew that at first the drilling would feel easy, and that he should go slowly through the spongy parts—it’s easy to push too far, too fast. Then as you get closer to the brain, it starts to feel as if you’re drilling through glass. That’s the best way he can explain it.

The implant was next to him, as sterile as he could make it. It was about the size of a paper clip—a platinum wire coated in silicone and plastic sheathing, bent at right angles, with electrodes attached to one end. He would use tweezers to stick it through the hole in his skull and into his sensory cortex…or maybe his motor cortex. It was hard to know exactly. Raduga had no medical training and little education after high school.

The implant, he believed, would help him have lucid dreams, the kind of dreams where you’re aware that you’re dreaming and can sometimes even determine what happens in them. He believed this so deeply that here he was, possibly about to die in an apartment in Kazakhstan if he slipped and drilled a millimeter too deep. He was doing this in the belief that if we could all have lucid dreams whenever we wanted, we would be happy—or at least endlessly entertained.

He pushed the drill carefully through the last bit of bone. He inserted the thing using the tweezers. He sutured the hole, which required the same degree of backward coordination. The surgery was done. It was certainly one of the riskier acts any human has ever performed in the name of scientific research, but he appeared to be alive.

Michael Raduga believes it was worth it. He believes he is a pioneer willing to risk his own life in the search for controllable dreams.

Other people believe he’s a fucking lunatic.

But isn’t that what they say about all the great ones?

Siberia, Around 2000

“IF YOU DON'T UNDERSTAND the reason I did the surgery, the rest of the story is complete insanity,” Raduga, 42, says. The reason he did the surgery, like the reasons any of us take our big swings in life, is rooted in his childhood, and indeed his childhood bedroom, where he was alone.

The apartment in Siberia, in which he had his first lucid dream, was small and cold. The floors were cold, hard wood, and the walls were blank. Raduga’s room held a small table, where he could do his schoolwork, and a bed with metal springs. There was a window. Raduga was a big, hulking kid who played sports every chance he could. The apartment could barely contain his size.

His boyhood spanned the waning years of the Cold War, some of its worst for the Russian people. Raduga hoped that when his mother returned home from work in the evenings she might be carrying a loaf of bread, but most often she was not. It was a scary time. Nuclear war felt perhaps more possible than ever, and there was talk of wars in space. As a child, Raduga developed a phobia that aliens would fly through the window of his small bedroom in the small apartment in Siberia and take him away.

He suffered from this for years. He was terrified of sleep. This kept up well into his late teens, even when he was working illegally as a nightclub bouncer. He was a bodybuilder, he boxed, he did karate—and he was scared of an alien abduction. He knew it was dumb—he was almost a man! Worrying about monsters from outer space!

But it was real, and he hated it.

Then, as desperate people sometimes do, he had an idea. He had read about phobias and learned that if you can confront a fear, you have a chance of overcoming it. But aliens aren’t like airplanes, or public speaking—you can’t just face them. But if he could somehow make himself dream about aliens, maybe he could stop being terrorized by the thought of them, thus regaining the thing he was missing most, the very thing he needed in order to execute his plan: sleep.

He tried and tried and tried. And one night, it worked. It worked beautifully, in fact, far beyond any hope he’d harbored. A creature entered his room through the window—it wasn’t a human form but a messy composite of aliens he had seen in movies. He rose from his bed, and the alien did not take him away. Eventually, it disappeared. Not only had Raduga had a dream about an alien coming for him and then leaving, but he had experienced what he would later understand was a lucid dream: While in a state of REM sleep, he had full consciousness, so the dream didn’t feel like a dream at all. Even after he woke up, it felt as if it had happened.

He felt that he had been in control.

The alien abduction phobia, he says, vanished from his life, in that he was liberated from it. In fact, he wanted to dream that dream again and again. “I was so fascinated because then I knew that there’s something else in this world,” he says. The alien had transformed from a symbol of fear to a suggestion of a better life. Where had it gone? he wondered. What else was out there? “I was dreaming about aliens hoping that they will abduct me, because I wanted to escape very harsh conditions in Siberia.”

The experience catalyzed what would first become Raduga’s avocation and then his vocation: a quest to help everyone have lucid dreams, so that everyone could feel what he did. He learned about how lucid dreaming could help people in other ways: spurring creativity, reducing PTSD and anxiety. He read books by Robert A. Monroe, a pioneer in the recognition and study of lucid dreams, whose 1971 book Journeys out of the Body spread the term “out-of-body experience”; Keith Hearne, who is often credited with establishing the first proof that lucid dreaming is real, in 1975; and Stephen LaBerge, a psychophysiologist and former Stanford University professor, who has worked on technological techniques for inducing a lucid dream state. He looked up studies and read about mysticism and paranormal esoterica. There were techniques out there: wake-induced lucid dreaming (WILD), for example, which involves waking up deliberately after several hours of sleep, then going back to sleep with the intention of having a lucid dream. Raduga even wrote a book himself at age 20, published in Russia, about his own early adventures in lucid dreaming. (“It was stupid, but very popular,” he says.)

He practiced bringing on lucid dreams, and it started working. Lucid dreaming begins with intention. Most practitioners suggest setting out to dream about a certain thing or person and concentrating only on that. Raduga sometimes induced dreams about his refrigerator being full of food—and himself eating it—because in post-Soviet Russia, it never was.

“I used it as a way to compensate for reality,” he says. “When a lucid dream happens, your sensations become as real as now, and sometimes even more real. You see someone and it is like you are seeing me right now. And it could be someone who is deceased, or someone you miss. It is hard to explain how everything could be more real than now. It is impossible to understand unless you have experienced it.”

But if everyone could experience this state of hyperreality? If everyone could activate what Raduga calls our internal AI, and control it, like virtual reality but far more real-feeling?

“In the future, you won’t be able to imagine your life without a device which can control your REM sleep,” he says. “It will be like the Internet. Or electricity. It will reshape our civilization. It will reshape our understanding of who we are.”

THE APARTMENT IN Kazakhstan was even smaller than the one in Siberia, a Soviet-era box with a bedroom, kitchen, and bathroom. But it was comfortable. And he was a grown man now, so he could buy food to put in the refrigerator.

After Siberia but before Kazakhstan, Raduga had been living and working in Novorossiysk, a town in southwestern Russia on the Black Sea, where he had set up a laboratory to study lucid dreaming. He hired two engineers and a sleep scientist, and he paid advanced lucid dreamers to participate in sleep studies. The rent on the lab (where he also lived) was the equivalent of only $500 a month, and he financed all of this by giving seminars and webinars and through donations from people who encouraged his work. He hadn’t graduated from college, yet he was publishing papers in research journals and planned to travel the world giving lessons and more seminars. He was a full-time dream researcher, not making money but able to live. Then, in February 2022, Vladimir Putin ordered the unprovoked invasion of Ukraine that begat a protracted, ugly, and deeply unpopular war. Some international companies ceased doing business with Russia, and the country became a pariah in every way, including in the scientific community. Many Western journals, Raduga says, simply stopped accepting papers from Russian scientists and researchers.

Just as important to his decision to leave Russia was his conclusion that the government had little interest in innovations that could benefit its own people, let alone all of humanity. “Russia is very stable in terms of being closed,” he says. “I thought Russia had changed and it would be open and I would develop something there, but it became clear to me during the war that it won’t change. It became obvious that I would never be able to do anything serious there. I need the whole world.”

He packed up his lab and moved to Almaty, Kazakhstan, the closest country he knew of with a United States embassy. His ultimate destination was Silicon Valley, where he knew dreams could come true. He applied for an EB-1B visa, a class of visa intended for people with “extraordinary abilities” that affords permanent residency in the U.S. While he waited for its approval, he could focus on his plan.

Which was a pretty stupid plan, and a reckless plan.

In the apartment, Raduga constructed the implant. Once it was in his brain, a second person, a sleep scientist, would press a button triggering the electrode to stimulate the brain at the perfect moment during REM sleep, potentially inducing lucid dreams.

Raduga thought about trying to hire a neurosurgeon to perform the surgery but soon realized it was unlikely that a doctor in Kazakhstan (or anywhere) would agree to insert the crude implant in his brain. Legally, ethically, and financially, it was not feasible.

He looked at himself in the mirror on the vanity in Almaty. “I was looking at myself, thinking about it,” he says. He had finished high school in Siberia and taken a few business courses at a university in Moscow, but that was the extent of his education. He had never taken so much as a CPR class, let alone the kind of medical training that would have taught him the first thing about carving into his head. And yet: “I looked at my place. It was just a crazy idea. Is it possible to do it by yourself? In reality, on a subconscious level, I immediately knew that I was going to do this.”

Raduga bought a drill used for metal engraving at a hardware store and a set of steel bits. He practiced on the sheep heads. He thought about the surgery for three months—worried over every detail, until eventually the idea became something he was used to. It was happening.

He was still nervous. He still knew he could die. But every surgery carries the risk of death.

In his mind, it became doable.

He set up an apparatus using an endoscope camera, the mirror, and a monitor, which enabled him to get the best view of the surgery site. He bought an anesthetic. He chose a day. He didn’t tell anyone what he was doing.

On the first try, he failed.

There was so much blood—he wasn’t prepared for that. Sheep heads were one thing, but cutting into his own flesh with a scalpel felt strange and uncomfortable, and he had to cut deeper and deeper, far deeper than he thought he would. It felt wrong. He stopped.

Could he do this?

“I was thinking that maybe I just didn’t have enough guts to do this,” he says. “I was fighting with myself: Am I really doing it wrong, or is it that I am searching for reasons to stop? Then in two days, I rethought everything. And I started the procedure again.”

He spent eight hours preparing, setting up the equipment, placing everything—the tools, the camera, the anesthetic, everything—where he could reach it without looking. He told a friend that if he didn’t hear from Raduga in two hours to come to the apartment. He didn’t say why.

The surgery took four hours and he estimates he lost at least 20 percent of his blood, which ended up on the floor and on his clothes and body. (He couldn’t get a transfusion because the whole surgery was a secret, so he focused on eating well and drinking fluids.) Raduga texted his friend every hour to let him know he was okay and that the guy didn’t need to come to the apartment. There was no research he knew of that suggested which part of the brain would be best; he was actually trying for the sensory cortex, which he hypothesized would be ideal, but missed and put it in the motor cortex.

After, Raduga looked around, exhausted. There was blood on the floor and a pile of bloody instruments next to him—bloody scalpel, bloody drill, bloody suture string, bloody tongs. And despite the exhaustion, he knew he shouldn’t go to sleep. Or rather, he was afraid to go to sleep.

He showered.

Improbably, he led an online webinar in lucid dreaming. He cleaned everything with water and bleach—stay busy, he thought.

And then, finally, he slept.

Redwood City, California, 2025

THE HOUSE IN Silicon Valley sits at the apex of a cul-de-sac. The sprawling main floor has multiple bedrooms, a large kitchen, and sliding doors that open onto a deck with 180-degree views of a valley of oaks and sweet gum trees. Raduga set up an outdoor gym at one end of the deck, outside his serene bedroom, which alone is about the size of the Almaty apartment.

Downstairs is for work. On the walls are pictures of scientists, inventors, and engineers—Einstein, Ivan Pavlov, Elon Musk. There are bedrooms designed for voluntary sleep studies, equipped with night-vision cameras and EEG machines. One large bedroom is set up as an office, where paid engineers who work for Raduga’s company, REMspace, come most days. He has 15 full-time employees and 10 part-time.

In the days following his DIY neurosurgery in Kazakhstan, Raduga walked into a private neurosurgery clinic and consulted with the surgeons about antibiotics and other medications he needed. He didn’t tell them he had performed the surgery on himself. In fact, he says they suspected some kind of malfeasance, perhaps that he had been drugged and dragged into a garage and someone had drilled into his skull to implant some nefarious device. Only after they removed it a month later did he tell the surgeons everything he had done, and was doing.

During that month, Raduga and a sleep scientist had studied how well the device could induce lucid dreams. The sleep scientist monitored his sleep using an EEG machine and would press a button to trigger an impulse through the electrode. They recorded everything—sleep patterns, eye movements, sleep paralysis, and brain waves, all of which can be used to show that a person is conscious while sleeping. But it was difficult. (Even in large, university-funded sleep labs, determining the “right” moment during REM sleep is extremely challenging.) One ironic obstacle was that discomfort from the surgery itself made it difficult for Raduga to fall into a deep enough sleep. During this time, Raduga had only two lucid dreams and one episode of sleep paralysis.

After the Daily Mail got word of Raduga’s surgery and news of it went viral, he began to gather a following of lucid dream enthusiasts around the world. Some sent him money to fund his research—mostly between $1,000 and $2,000, but one investor who contacted him ended up investing $800,000, Raduga says. He “had no idea” how funding worked in the United States, but living in Silicon Valley, he studied up and learned quickly. He is currently meeting with actual venture capital firms about a new investment round.

(He has heard plenty of no’s, and some people have told him they think he’s crazy. When he applied for his green card, he was subjected to two medical exams and an extra psychiatric evaluation he was told was not part of the normal protocol. He says there was no diagnosis of mental illness.)

A primary goal of REMspace, which he launched in August 2023, is to create technology that can induce lucid dreams without having to drill a hole in your skull and stuff an electrode into your cranium. Earlier this year, the company started selling a mask, which it calls LucidMe, for $285 and which looks from the outside like a normal sleep mask you’d see in a hotel. (It’s available on the REMspace website.) An accompanying LucidMe app purports to operate the mask and log when you’ve had lucid dreams.

The mask doesn’t emit electrical impulses like Raduga’s implant did; rather, it detects REM sleep, then sends simple signals—lights, vibrations—designed to stir users to consciousness without actually awakening them, the basic definition of a lucid dream. Interrupting sleep at the right moment is key to inducing a lucid dream. The goal is to do this without waking the user up, something previous lucid-dream “technology” has relied on. Raduga admits that the current version of LucidMe often fully awakens users; it’s already being phased out to make way for its next version, which will include three times the number of sensors and, he hopes, take the revolutionary step of inducing lucid dreams that are far more visceral and far longer—and more controllable—than anything to date. (The new mask is still in the testing phase.)

The company’s website, REMspace.net, is full of you-can-do-it! encouragement and simple explanations. It seems designed and written to entice the first-timer. A kind of FAQ page begins with the question “What is ‘lucid dreaming’ in not-so-complicated words?” Answer: “Lucid Dreaming is like a special condition where you suddenly realize, ‘Oh, I am inside my dream!’ At that point, you can change the story of what is happening. Imagine you stand in a cinema hall—no, not only watching a movie, but actually messing around with its storyline, like you are the big main character inside the film! Maybe it sounds too ridiculous, yes? But for many people, this is [sic] normal situation.”

(Stephen LaBerge, for his part, once described lucid dreaming in a speech at Stanford as simply “a dream in which you know you are dreaming while it is happening.”)

Raduga’s site sells not only the LucidMe but his 2014 book, The Phase: Shattering the Illusion of Reality, which was previously titled The Phase: A Practical Guidebook for Lucid Dreaming and Out-of-Body Travel. It has 166 reviews, mostly four- and five-star.

There is a “How To” section on the site, offering free videos and tutorials, beginning with what most students of lucid dreaming seem to agree is the first step: creating intention for what you hope to do or see in your dream. A list of possible goals follows, including “travel around the planet and space,” “meet celebrities and loved ones,” and “adult entertainment (18+).” A note below advises that your odds of achieving a lucid dream increase if you go in with two or three such goals.

Online reviews of the LucidMe, on YouTube, social media, and some websites that cover lucid dreaming, are as erratic as opinions about lucid dreaming itself tend to be. “First mask that actually got me out of body,” says a guy on TikTok. Many others have reported similar success. Others have complained about its effectiveness and price.

While the language on the site can sound fantastical in places (“At REMspace Inc., we believe that REM sleep will be the next groundbreaking advancement following AI, poised to revolutionize civilization”) and hornswoggling in others (“This is a universal and most effective way to obtain lucid dreams and out-of-body experience. Success rate for 1–5 attempts (1–3 days) is around 50%!”) and is sometimes clumsily written, there’s a ton of it. It’s thorough and, overall, earnest. The belief and passion behind the operation are obviously real.

The Future

RADUGA DID OPERATE on his own brain with the hardware store drill, and while that single action has made him an LD hero to some, it has also made him a deeply controversial figure in the field. And his claim that we can all buy his mask and stand a 50 percent chance of experiencing a lucid dream within three nights of use—in which we might see a dead relative, have sex with someone we want to have sex with, or fill a refrigerator with food—runs counter to what some of the foremost researchers and practitioners in the field say.

Deirdre Leigh Barrett, PhD, is a lecturer in the psychology department at Harvard Medical School and the editor in chief of Dreaming, the widely respected journal of the International Association for the Study of Dreams. She first encountered Raduga’s presurgery research when he was at his lab in Novorossiysk, and she published in Dreaming several studies he coauthored. She characterizes him as, at the time, “an author on some quite solid lucid dream research—none of which has anything to do with anything you wear at night that technologically produces more lucid dreams.”

Barrett suspects that a technological device of some kind might one day show success at inducing lucid dreams, but not anytime soon. (“And I would be surprised if it was Raduga’s group that figured it out,” she says.) The problem is that trying to engineer the perfect scenario for a lucid dream to occur is challenging even in the most controlled setting. “The idea of trying to artificially activate it without waking the person up or otherwise knocking them out of REM sleep is a very plausible strategy to try to induce lucid dreaming,” Barrett says. “But the big labs that have done that with good EEG equipment and absolutely state-of-the-art, tens of thousands of dollars’ worth of magnetic or electrical stimulation devices have not succeeded in producing lucid dreams.”

“I did not think of his self-surgery as research and thought he was lucky to survive it,” she says. (She adds: “Some of my colleagues think it was faked and not real, but I tend to believe he really did it.” Raduga sent Men’s Health footage of the surgery and says he can prove he did it “at any moment to anyone.”) Barrett does not recommend buying LucidMe or any of the other masks on the market that promise similar success.

More than one scientist I spoke with bemoaned that this article was being written at all, given Raduga’s methods and what they consider his ill-gotten fame. Karen Konkoly holds a PhD in psychology from Northwestern University, where she was one of the first in the United States in several decades to focus her doctoral research on the neuroscience of lucid dreaming. She’s a dream scientist and serves as the content lead for DUST, a dream tech start-up, and she met Raduga while on a corporate retreat this year. She liked him and admired his passion, but she worries that any attention on him is attention not focused on research that’s more scientifically rigorous.

“Take lucid dreaming research seriously,” she says, sounding almost pleading. “On one hand, the publicity is kind of good for the field, but on the other hand, it’s also crazy. I have a vlog about lucid dreaming where I do studies and I make a little vlog. And [Raduga publicizing his surgery] would be like if I did a vlog study on myself and then sent a press release to the media saying, ‘I did this.’ But his [surgery] didn’t even touch the scientific process. It’s one thing to do that, but it’s frustrating as a scientist who did do all that and had my study peer-reviewed for five years—as opposed to just trying one thing, making a weird video about it, and then sending it to the media and having it blow up into this viral event. It wasn’t even a study, and people don’t know the difference.”

There is even some disagreement about whether REM sleep is indeed when most of our dreams happen—and “even detecting REM sleep in real time is a technical challenge,” says Konkoly. And yet research into how to induce lucid dreams is only growing.

Konkoly helped develop a technique called targeted lucidity reactivation, in which sounds are repeatedly paired with a lucid mindset (paying attention to your experiences and evaluating them in detail) before sleep and can trigger lucid dreams. She and Barrett also both mentioned galantamine, an FDA-approved drug for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease, as a popular way to try to have lucid dreams.

But the whole idea of insta-lucidity may itself be only a dream. Daniel Love is a British oneirologist (oneirology being a subfield of psychology that specializes in the scientific study of dreams) and lucid-dreaming teacher who has consulted for the BBC and written books that encourage lucid dreaming while tending to temper expectations. Lucid dreams, he says, when achieved, are subject to invasive thoughts and mind wandering just like regular dreams and, in fact, our normal waking life.

“The subconscious mind is a mess—it’s a fascinating mess,” he says. “And for the vast majority of people on the planet, and possibly all, we’re nowhere near as in control of our minds as we’d like to believe we are. Therefore, controlling lucid dreams is a bit of a red herring.”

As for any pitch that a device or “easy” method could have you dreaming any lucid dream you like within a few tries, he says, “Lucid dreaming is an interesting altered state of consciousness which allows for some degree of control, some degree of exploration, but even if you could switch that state on for everyone magically, that wouldn’t guarantee perfect dream control. It wouldn’t be an Xbox of the mind.”

Love is clear that while he heard about Raduga’s self-surgery on the news, he is not deeply familiar with his research. (On Love’s site, The Lucid Guide, a biography of him includes the line “Love is known as an outspoken opponent of those who spread misinformation regarding lucid dreaming or profiteer through the sale of misleading content or products.” But he did not comment to Men’s Health on Raduga’s work specifically.)

Raduga still has a hole in his skull. He always will. It’s covered by tissue now and his sandy, close-cropped hair. There’s no bone underneath. He inserted a titanium plate into a baseball cap that he wears anyplace he might get poked in the head.

He is focused on perfecting the LucidMe and the technology he says enables it to induce and chronicle dreams. “Why can’t we all have lucid dreams right now? Because right now it is hard to induce lucid dreams even if you know all the methods,” Raduga says. “People are not ready to invest so much time, even though you know that it’s cool. But when you have to read books, you think, Okay, my life is fine without it. And this future, which I have shared with you, will never exist without technology which would help people to induce lucid dreams easily.”

He is impatient with what he says is the slow pace of research into lucid dreams and hopes LucidMe will catapult it forward by collecting data from millions of new lucid dreamers. He knows his surgery was ill-advised even if it did help him have lucid dreams, and he wouldn’t do it again. But if it does lead him to perfecting a device that anyone can buy and use and perchance experience what he says he did when the alien came into his bedroom in Siberia?

They’ll come. They’ll buy it. They’ll use it.

Then they’ll dream. And to Raduga, it will all have been worth it.
