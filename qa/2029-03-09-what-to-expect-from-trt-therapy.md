Starting TRT: The First Steps

Hormones play a critical role in growth, development, and regulate numerous health processes. The overproduction, absence, or deficiency of hormones leads to hormonal imbalance which can result in a range of health complications.

Testosterone, a sex steroidal hormone, produced primarily in the testes is a critical health regulator in those assigned male at birth (AMAB). Those who suffer from lowered testosterone levels are at risk of muscle loss, low libido, and developing chronic heart conditions.

If you believe you’re experiencing testosterone deficiency, you may be curious as to your treatment options. Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) has been clinically used as a treatment for low testosterone for roughly 70 years.1

Having an idea of what to expect from testosterone can help you weigh the potential of moving forward with TRT.

Initial Doses and Administration Methods

Treating testosterone deficiency through TRT involves replacing the hormone with a synthetic bioidentical version of testosterone. This addresses low testosterone by increasing serum testosterone levels and the way it can be administered varies. Current and common forms of testosterone are categorized by either topical or injected administration methods. Oral supplements are not an FDA-approved form of administration as they have been shown to rapidly metabolize, leading to testosterone fluctuations and liver complications.

If you’re considering TRT, there are multiple well-studied options, including topical gels, patches, implants and injections. Initial dosage will depend heavily on your physiological deficiency and administration method. For example, most gels are initially prescribed at ~50 mg daily, while injections can range from a dose of 100-750 mg per month.

Common Side Effects

Each type of TRT has its own potential side effects related to the method of administration, including injection or implant site pain, itching, or skin irritation when using topical gels and patches. Additionally, there are common early side effects associated with the general use of testosterone to look out for, such as an increase in acne and mood swings.

First Month on TRT

The first month of TRT will be an extension of the first week with similar symptoms and added effects resulting from more time on testosterone.

Physical and Emotional Adjustments

After more time using TRT, you can expect further changes in both emotions and energy. A noticeable and often highly desired change is increased libido. Decreased sex drive can be a disturbance for many people and is often a main indicator of testosterone deficiency.

Additionally, those experiencing low levels of testosterone can experience increased body fat, decreased lean mass, and decreased muscle mass. Research has linked prolonged TRT usage to physical changes, including more optimal body composition from increased muscle mass.

Monitoring Progress

The first month after starting TRT can be overwhelming. Along with expected emotional and physical changes, you may be experiencing unwanted results you were not prepared for. These adverse effects could be due to factors like drug interactions as well as too little or too much testosterone.

Testosterone treatment requires consistent and open communication with a healthcare provider. Follow-up appointments and blood tests are important checkpoints for progress monitoring. This allows for you and your doctor to properly and timely respond to any impacts from starting TRT.

Addressing Potential Issues

Managing the long term side effects of TRT requires addressing the potential issues that extended testosterone use can cause. Elevated testosterone is associated with thickening of muscle cells, and cardiac muscle is no exception, which can make it difficult for the heart to pump blood efficiently. Studies show that cardiac hypertrophy can result from higher levels of testosterone.

Additionally, research has found that increased testosterone is also linked to reduced testes’ size, and lowered semen efficacy in those AMAB. Further research needs to be done to better understand the effects of continued TRT, especially in those with preexisting conditions like sleep apnea, erythrocytosis, and prostate cancer. Individuals desiring to preserve fertility or who may be concerned with acute complications due to elevated testosterone levels or should investigate alternatives to TRT.
